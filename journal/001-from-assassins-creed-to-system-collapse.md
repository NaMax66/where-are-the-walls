# 001 — From Assassin's Creed to system collapse

## The accidental beginning

This investigation did not begin as a research project.

It began with *Assassin's Creed Odyssey* and an interest in the ruins behind the game's Greek world. That led backwards from Classical Greece toward Mycenaeans and Minoans, and eventually to Knossos.

Then one simple question appeared:

> **Where are the walls?**

A wealthy Bronze Age society had large palace centers, storerooms, administration, craft production, art, long-distance contacts and enough organizational capacity to build complex architecture. Yet the major Minoan centers did not look like Mycenae or Tiryns: there was no obvious massive defensive shell around them.

The absence was more interesting than another list of Minoan achievements.

If the society had resources worth taking, why could it remain so open?

The usual word — *thalassocracy*, security through command of the sea — was not really an explanation. It merely moved the question one step outward. What kind of system would make walls less necessary?

That became the first anomaly.

---

## Stop looking at Crete alone

The next move was to stop asking only what archaeologists found on Crete and ask what people living at the same time said about the island and its inhabitants.

This moved the investigation into the archives of other Bronze Age societies.

### Kaptara

Texts from Mari mention **Kaptara/Kaptaru**, commonly associated with Crete. More interesting than the name itself was the context: Kaptaran people and goods appear inside a much larger commercial and political world.

One especially provocative detail was the appearance of a Kaptaran alongside an **interpreter connected with the chief merchant of the Kaptarans at Ugarit**.

That changed the mental picture.

Instead of an isolated island civilization occasionally sending exotic pots overseas, it suggested people embedded in an international network: merchants, interpreters, commercial representatives, routes and relationships.

The interpreter mattered almost more than the goods. An interpreter is evidence of a function. Someone needs to cross a linguistic boundary often enough for translation to become a role.

### Keftiu

Egyptian evidence supplied another view. People identified with **Keftiu** and the Aegean appear in Egyptian elite imagery carrying luxury vessels whose forms strongly resemble objects known from the Minoan world.

The Egyptian scenes are not transparent photographs of international relations — Egyptian royal ideology shapes what is shown — but they reinforce a larger point: Crete was visible from outside as part of a high-level exchange system.

At this point the question was no longer simply:

> Why did Knossos have no walls?

It was becoming:

> **What function did Crete perform inside the wider Bronze Age system?**

---

## The first economic model: Crete as a production node

Crete did not possess all of the strategic raw materials required by a Bronze Age economy. Copper and especially tin had to move through long-distance networks. Archaeological finds of metal ingots and other imported materials made it possible to ask a more specific question:

> Could Crete have imported relatively basic strategic inputs and exported higher-value products, services or designs?

That produced a tentative model:

**imported raw materials → specialized Cretan processing and craft → high-value goods → international distribution**

The modern analogy that came to mind was **Taiwan**: an island that can become disproportionately important not because it owns every raw material, but because a difficult-to-reproduce concentration of knowledge, production capacity, relationships and logistics develops there.

Another analogy was **Apple**. The value of Apple cannot be reconstructed by capturing one building. Much of the system exists in people, procedures, design competence, suppliers, standards, distribution and relationships. The headquarters is only a visible node in a larger architecture.

These are metaphors, not evidence. But they changed which archaeological evidence seemed worth looking for.

If Crete really worked this way, there should be traces not merely of Cretan objects abroad, but of Cretan **people and operating presence** abroad.

---

## Looking for the people

The next prediction was straightforward:

> If Crete was deeply involved in international commerce, perhaps foreign merchant communities should also be visible on Crete.

This prediction did not work especially well.

There is evidence of mobility and foreign connections, but a clear network of permanent Levantine merchant quarters in Neopalatial Crete is difficult to demonstrate.

That was useful precisely because it weakened the first version of the model.

Instead of discarding the whole idea, the asymmetry itself became interesting.

What if the network did not work as an open cosmopolitan marketplace in which everyone established equivalent communities everywhere?

What if the Cretan core was relatively closed while Cretans themselves operated outward?

This led to another search: Minoan communities and strongly Minoanized settlements outside Crete.

Places such as **Kastri on Kythera, Miletus, Trianda on Rhodes, and several Cycladic centers** showed different degrees of Minoan presence, migration, local production, cult practice, writing and cultural integration.

A rough gradient became imaginable:

**Cretan core → permanent communities and settlements in the Aegean → deeply integrated local centers → merchant representatives at distant hubs → diplomatic/commercial partners farther inland**

Again Apple provided a useful metaphor: a protected internal production culture combined with external stores, representatives and interfaces distributed across important markets.

The model was becoming less like a territorial empire and more like a network.

---

## Returning to the walls

Now the original anomaly could be revisited.

Perhaps walls were not absent because Minoans were mysteriously peaceful or because nobody had thought of attacking them.

Perhaps some of their security was a **property of the system**.

If a node provides valuable logistics, production, distribution or mediation functions to powerful neighbors, destroying it may be more expensive than trading with it.

That does not make the node safe from everyone.

A major state participating in the system may understand the value of keeping the node alive. A raider looking for immediate loot does not necessarily internalize that system-level value.

This suggested a new distinction:

**systemic security is not the same as local military security.**

That made scattered defensive sites, watchpoints or later fortification pressure potentially compatible with largely unfortified palace centers.

It also suggested a test: compare the chronology of Cretan defensive infrastructure with the growth of mainland warrior elites and maritime capability. If defensive investment rises as a new class of actors becomes capable of reaching Crete, the pattern would support the model. If the chronology does not fit, the model weakens.

The walls had turned from an absence into a systems question.

---

## Optimization has a price

At this point the discussion moved beyond Crete.

The eastern Mediterranean Bronze Age was clearly not a collection of fully independent boxes. Metals, luxury goods, diplomatic gifts, ships, merchants, specialists and information moved between regions.

Once a network becomes sufficiently integrated, specialization becomes attractive.

A node that can rely on other nodes does not need to reproduce every capability internally. It can become exceptionally good at a smaller set of functions.

That creates a reinforcing loop:

**trade → specialization → higher productivity → more trade → deeper specialization**

But optimization has a price.

> **Efficiency can increase while autonomy decreases.**

This immediately suggested another dimension: **redundancy**.

Dependency alone is not necessarily dangerous. A function can depend on external inputs and still be robust if there are many interchangeable suppliers and alternative routes. Fragility appears when optimization removes substitutes, concentrates knowledge, or creates single points of failure.

So the vague label **"complex society"** began to look unsatisfactory.

"Complex" says that many things are happening. It does not tell us how the system works.

A more useful vocabulary seemed to be:

- specialization;
- connectivity;
- centralization;
- substitutability;
- redundancy;
- autonomy;
- reproducibility.

This was the point where the unit of analysis began to change from **things** to **functions**.

---

## Functions, not objects

A palace, a tablet, a scribe or a ship is an object or role we can observe.

But the system cares about what those things **do**.

For example, Linear B writing can be reframed as part of a function such as **administrative memory and coordination**.

Its implementation required more than symbols scratched into clay:

**palace demand + trained scribes + teaching + procedures + records + administrative routines**

This leads to an important distinction:

> **A function is not the same thing as its implementation.**

People still needed shelter after palace collapse even if monumental architecture disappeared. People still needed memory and agreements even if Linear B disappeared. A function can survive by changing implementation, while a particular highly optimized implementation can vanish.

This suggested several possible post-collapse outcomes:

- a function survives almost unchanged;
- a function survives but at reduced scale;
- a function survives through a different implementation;
- a function disappears and later returns;
- a function disappears for centuries.

Suddenly collapse looked less like a single event and more like an experiment performed on a dependency graph.

---

## The reversal

Initially the reasoning ran in the normal direction:

**complex interconnected system → specialization → dependency → vulnerability → collapse**

Then the direction reversed.

Suppose we do not know the architecture of the original system very well — which is exactly the archaeological problem.

But we can observe what happens when the system is disrupted.

Some functions survive.

Some degrade.

Some change implementation.

Some disappear almost immediately.

Some do not return for centuries.

That pattern contains information about the architecture that existed before the collapse.

Linear B is an extreme example. Palace administration disappears and the writing system disappears with it. Greek writing later reappears through a different technological and institutional system.

This suggests that Linear B literacy was not simply a distributed skill possessed by "society". Its reproduction was deeply coupled to the palace system that needed, trained and supported scribes.

Agriculture behaves differently. Ordinary household production behaves differently. Oral culture behaves differently.

The contrast is the evidence.

And this produced the central formulation of the project:

> **The pattern of lost functions is a fingerprint of the destroyed system.**

Or, more explicitly:

> The selective extinction, degradation, survival and replacement of functions after a collapse may allow us to infer the specialization, redundancy and dependency structure that existed before the collapse.

The collapse becomes an accidental **chaos-engineering test**.

If AWS disappeared tomorrow and banks, shops, transport systems and government services failed in different combinations and at different speeds, an observer who never saw the architecture diagrams could still infer hidden dependencies from the failure pattern.

Archaeology faces a similar problem, only with a gap of thousands of years.

---

## What changed during the investigation

Several ideas became weaker or changed form along the way.

The absence of walls was initially tempting to explain with a simple "Minoan naval supremacy" story. That proved too vague.

The idea of a production and trade hub initially suggested obvious foreign merchant quarters on Crete. The evidence for that prediction was weak, which pushed the model toward a potentially asymmetric network rather than a simple cosmopolitan marketplace.

The idea of a Minoan "empire" became less useful as evidence of different kinds of presence abroad suggested a gradient rather than uniform territorial control.

The Bronze Age collapse initially appeared as the end of the story. It became instead a source of information about the story that came before it.

Most importantly, the word **complexity** became less satisfying. Calling something a complex system can hide the very structure we want to understand.

The emerging question is not:

> How complex was this civilization?

but:

> **Which functions existed, where were they performed, what did they depend on, how replaceable were they, and which parts of the system could reproduce themselves when the network broke?**

---

## Where the project is now

The project does not yet know what it is ultimately about.

It may remain a study of **Minoan Crete**.

It may become a comparative study of **civilizations and collapse**.

It may become an investigation of **systems, specialization and resilience** using archaeological cases.

Or the research process itself may become part of the subject: how a question encountered in a video game — *where are the walls?* — can lead through archaeology, trade, writing and collapse toward a general way of thinking about systems.

For now, choosing among those would be premature.

The useful thing is to preserve the path while continuing to test it.

## Next questions

1. Can the chronology of Cretan defensive infrastructure be compared with the rise of mainland maritime and warrior power?
2. Which functions demonstrably disappear, degrade, survive or change implementation across the end of the Bronze Age?
3. Can we define a useful measure of a function's autonomy or reproducibility?
4. Are there archaeological studies that already use selective post-collapse functional loss as a reverse proxy for prior dependency?
5. Can the method produce correct predictions in another collapse — and, more importantly, where does it produce false ones?

The last question matters most. A metaphor becomes a model only when it can fail.
